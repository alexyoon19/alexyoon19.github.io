Why a Donkey’s Death Is Beautiful

Choreographing the Cinematograph Instead of Actors: How Balthazar’s Death Scene Derives its Beauty and Significance

May 2022

After a life of callous treatment, Balthazar, old and wounded, lies down in a meadow full of sheep to peacefully die. With the sun shining and the occasional bleating of sheep, nearly every critic and cinephile regards this scene as the most beautiful scene in Au Hasard Balthazar, perhaps even the most arresting in all of Bresson’s filmography. However, Bresson’s own view stands in stark contrast to this conventional view; a film review states that the director viewed the death “as the prolonged expiry of an old, abused animal, too wounded to bray, too exhausted to do anything but collapse to the earth, his value depleted.”1 As such, there is significant dissonance between the audience’s impressions of the scene and the director’s own intent and interpretation. We cannot help but wonder whether we are viewing the film “incorrectly” when our experience differs from creator intent. Nevertheless, on a deeper understanding of how Bresson views cinematic art and how he seeks to present it to the viewer, we can begin to understand the discrepancy.

In his Notes on Cinematography, Bresson states that “cinematographic film, where the images, like the words in a dictionary, have no power and value except through their position and relation.”2 Thus, the final scene in Au Hasard Balthazar gains its beauty not through its inherent nature, but because we watch it cinematically. Specifically, through contrasting perceptual framing of animal characters on screen, temporal ellipsis, Balthazar’s spatial positioning in the context of the narrative, and atypical auditory qualities, Bresson inevitably sets up for the final scene to be interpreted as beautiful, as we necessarily obtain its “expression…by relations of images and of sounds.”3

Perhaps the most directly impactful scene that we compare Balthazar’s death scene to is the scene depicting Balthazar’s introduction to the traveling circus’s animals — his only prolonged interaction with other characters of similar biological kind. We observe Balthazar, essentially shackled in chains, being moved through the circus’s animal housing cages. He first comes face-to-face with a tiger, and long-held shots of Balthazar’s face and the tiger’s face are presented to the audience. There is no movement or much noise; however, a tacit conversation seemingly takes place through the filming of the two animals’ eyes. Bresson trains his camera onto a diagonal shot of Balthazar’s face, cutting to the tiger with a head-on shot after a few seconds. The camera flips back and forth between these cross-cuts. No dialogue is present, but through the perceptual presentation of the face and eyes in alternating, close-up shots, Bresson creates the cinematic environment typically present when filming dialogue. The eyes of each animal, if taken at face value, would be simple biological apparatuses needed for vision, but by prolonging the shots on them, the audience is left to see emotions in them. Perhaps we see pity and empathy on Balthazar’s part, or sadness and dejection on the tiger’s part. Subsequent shots are very similar, but Bresson continues to zoom into the animals’ faces, accentuating every movement made. Balthazar has such silent conversations with a polar bear, a chimp, and an elephant. Compassion is communicated through silent, shared suffering, almost scientifically magnified by the sustained, close-up camera shots. No unnatural panning shots are used, because such movement “do not cor­respond to the movements of the eye. This is to separate the eye from the body.”4 As such, the scene depicts captive animals — the invisible victims of human cruelty, stripped of their agency — sharing pure, natural thoughts without language, recognized due to the cinematic medium.

The next time we are introduced to a slew of animals along with Balthazar is during his death scene. As these two scenes are the only prolonged sequences in the film in which Balthazar interacts with other animals, we directly juxtapose their differences in perceptual framing of body parts, camera cuts, and shot organization. In contrast to the tightly shot, abruptly changing, zoomed-in frames of the circus scene, the final sequence flows much smoother and peacefully. Soft sheep nuzzle against Balthazar, who gently lies down on the grass. In place of intently focusing in on his eyes or face, Bresson opts to slowly zoom out, drawing the camera from a close-up of Balthazar’s bullet wound to a wider shot of his whole body, subsequently employing a panning shot to follow his entire body as he trots across the pasture. The camera no longer employs frequent cuts, nor does it rest on animals’ faces for an uncomfortably long period of time. Instead, it slowly descends onto the freely roaming sheep, with Balthazar in a seeming state of peace; after lying down on the meadow, his eyes — which previously carried sympathy through collective suffering — slowly begin to close, almost as if he is nodding off to sleep, not dying in pain.

The contrasting presentation of the animals — distinct from our human characters — create the necessary relation between scenes. These sequences only contain animals, which is important to highlight, because communication to the audience can only be done through visual perception and editing due to the absence of dialogue and intelligible sounds. It is distinctly Bressonian; he completely removes “the terrible habit of theater” from the screen, rather tying “new relationships between persons and things which are, and as they are.”5 These animals are the quintessential Bressonian “models.” They gain their sentience not from emotional acting, but the cinematic presentation itself. Thus, even though the audience may not explicitly call back to the circus scene while watching Balthazar’s death, the two are inextricably tied to each other due to their intense cinematographic differences that push the audience to understand why such differences are pursued. Gently wandering sheep that are filmed panoramically is much more comforting to the audience than a caged tiger communicating with Balthazar through abrupt camera cuts. Bresson knows this.

As Bresson says, “images and sounds must sustain one another, from far and from near. No independent images or sounds.”6 Again, while the audience may not be consciously relating the two scenes, Balthazar’s death would resonate differently without the inclusion of the circus scene. As such, by distinguishing the circus scene with all its restrictiveness, frequent cuts, and excruciating biological detail in depicting animals against the rather free, visually smooth, and continuously filmed sheep and Balthazar in the death scene inject the latter sequence with superior aesthetic beauty. Both scenes contain animals that Balthazar communicates with — however, the cinematic medium injects the earlier scene with the silent communication of shared sadness, especially with camera angle normally used in filming dialogue and close-up shots. As such, the cinematic medium controls the audience’s immersion in both the scene setting and character interaction, establishing a reference point of animal communication in the film. Thus, when we are confronted with another scene in which animals communicate silently, we cannot help but notice the difference in camera angles and perceptual framing of body parts on screen. Rather having one-on-one wordless conversations with caged tigers and monkeys, Balthazar is now freer, interacting with roaming sheep in a natural environment. The framing of the content on screen is more visually pleasing — rather than capturing a pained elephant’s eye in grotesque detail, the audience is immersed in the natural movement of the sheep and Balthazar’s peace. His eyes, which seemed to relay pity and sympathy before, is void of any negative emotion. In this respect, Balthazar’s death scene derives its beauty from its dependency on previous images and presentations — the remnants of the circus scene, stimulate us “with relations of images that render them both alive and moving.”7 Without it, there would be less reason for us to think of Balthazar’s final moments as particularly special; we would not have the knowledge nor a reason to “sanctify” it in our minds.

As shown, perceptual presentation — objects shown through the camera in particular perspectives and frames — push the audience towards a beautiful interpretation of the final scene. Nevertheless, as with almost any of Bresson’s films, temporal organization is further used as a similar medium, manipulated throughout the film to “hide the ideas, but so that people find them.”8 This technique is implemented to enrich our final scene once again. In Au Hasard Balthazar, such an ellipsis is most apparent in the scenes leading up to Balthazar’s death — beginning with Marie’s assault scene, in which Gerard and his mischievous friends beat, strip, (presumably) rape, and lock her into an abandoned house for wanting to end her relationship with Gerard. While it is one of the most significant scenes in the film both in terms of impact on the narrative, we are never shown the attack itself, the faces of the boys, Marie’s sensory reactions to the attack, nor the spatial surroundings of the scene. One minute, Marie is in the hallway of the abandoned house, wearily looking side-to-side; next moment, the boys are throwing her clothes up into the air, cheering as they run away from the house. Marie, shaking and traumatized, is curled up in the corner, unable to even look at her father.

Such an ellipsis is important, because it forces us to focus on what is actually important to Bresson: Balthazar’s role in Marie’s life. Showing the assault, while potentially viscerally impactful, would detract from the pure connection that exists between Balthazar and Marie, diluting the former’s significance in the conflicts at play. Specifically, Bresson seeks to show that when in each other’s company, abuse and confusion is absent for both. Thus, Bresson, focusing only on the donkey’s legs and a strewn piece of Marie’s clothing on the ground, introduces Balthazar in the shot immediately following Gerard and his friends’ departure from the scene of the assault. The shot does not initially show the bystanders peering through the window at Marie, nor does is depict Marie’s worried father, racing to save his child. Balthazar is the first to arrive on the scene — he is the one carrying Marie’s father and will eventually be the one to carry her out of the setting. Effectively, the donkey is a savior. There is intentional effort on Bresson’s part to focus on Balthazar’s legs at the beginning of the scene, forcing the audience to look at the mechanism that stably connects Balthazar to earth. It is a luxury that Marie cannot afford, due to her attack — Balthazar must once more lend his body out to care for Marie, carrying her to safety.

We then learn of Marie’s death (or permanent escape from home). It is another temporal ellipsis employed by Bresson; the death is not shown on screen, and we do not know how much time has passed since her assault. Subsequently, her father’s dies, and Balthazar is once more asked to carry the body on his back for the funeral procession. We are deliberately excluded from understanding the exact passage of time as it relates to the narrative. We are oblivious as to how much time has passed since Marie’s assault to Marie’s death/disappearance to Marie’s father’s death. However, there is a constant in all of these scenes: Balthazar’s role as a fundamental rescuer and comforter in each of the scenarios, as he is present both before and after each of the tragedies. With the ellipses, Bresson can more clearly and directly connect the idea that to Marie’s family, Balthazar is the only figure that can bear their suffering and provide some sense of comfort for the family members. The audience is cut off from information that would better explain the predicament of each human character, which would detract focus from Balthazar; we are continuously shown a stoic Balthazar, carrying out his apparent duty to deliver each of Marie’s family members to peace and safety. Such messages are conveyed literally as he carries Marie back to safety and Marie’s father to the afterlife and figuratively in that he is seen as a saint in the mother’s eyes: “He's old. He's all I have. Besides...he's a saint.” Absence of information and depriving time information to the audience is employed as a tool to urge the audience to more immediately consider Balthazar’s saint-like status in Marie’s household after each family member’s death or tragedy.

Thus, in the next sequences, where Balthazar is forcibly removed from Marie’s home by Gerard and his friends and taken to unwillingly participate in a smuggling operation, the audience is much more likely to view such an act as morally wrong — perhaps even impious. A saint has been removed from his home to be used for a wicked, criminal act. Now, while Marie’s assault nor Marie’s death was shown on screen, the entirety of the smuggling operation is shown, from beginning to its eventual failure. In this manner, even after being treated callously and repeatedly tortured for almost the entirety of the movie like Marie, Balthazar does not have the cinematic luxury that his human companion had in the previous scenes — namely, no ellipsis is employed. Bresson does not dismiss his final moments relatively painlessly and non-graphically as he does with Marie and Marie’s father. Rather, Bresson elects to capture every moment that leads up to his death in great detail. We are shown a closeup of his stoic face once again, now abandoned by Gerard’s gang, as each gunshot rings throughout the frames of the film until one inevitably strikes Balthazar. It is one of the rare exhibitions of emotion from Balthazar that we witness.

At this point in the film, with ellipses continuously reminding the audience of Balthazar’s selfless nobility, it is almost impossible for us to view the death as simply a donkey dying after being depleted of its value. Such a reading would clash with the relationships of images that Bresson has cultivated, even just over the final ten minutes of the film. We are forced to watch Balthazar — a figure that Bresson labels as a saint — bear the burden of Marie’s family’s suffering and pain; thus, when he himself begins to die, the images are more similar to an angel returning to heaven than a nameless animal taking its final breaths. With the ellipses, Bresson has consciously decided to leave the passage of time and the exact unfolding of events as ambiguous in Marie’s assault and Marie’s father’s grief, which has facilitated both the accentuation of Balthazar’s guardian angel status in their lives and his own pain. Through ordering and manipulating the temporal dimension of the film into this precise sequence, Bresson has inserted a sacred power into the frames that Balthazar appears in — this oeuvre is unmistakably present at the end of the film. The simple insertion and deletion of information symbolically anthropomorphizes an animal character with saint-like characteristics. Instead of explicitly stating this sentiment to the audience, Bresson has clearly advertised the point through a craftier lens: “don’t run after poetry. It penetrates unaided through the joins (ellipses).”9

Fittingly, Au Hasard Balthazar does not only make use of the one-dimensional time aspect to beautify Balthazar’s death scene, but also the three remaining dimensions of space within the narrative, cinematically choreographing all four dimensions of human experience to connect with the character conflict. The amount of space that Balthazar fills on screen, as well as the physical positioning of his body around other characters throughout the film, again build an unspoken association in the audience’s mind — that Balthazar is a stoic, unflappable servant, accepting the physical cruelty and moral barbarity of humans. He frequently finds himself in the middle of human drama, and his physical placement within the context of the setting, among other shots, and within dialogue amplifies its effect on later scenes. Accordingly, we can parallel such discernible images to Balthazar’s death scene once again, due to the similarities in physical placement but vastly divergent intellectual effect. We see multiple scenes in which the donkey finds himself caught as an unrelated victim of deteriorating relationships between two humans. Such predicaments are exemplified especially in depictions of the abusive Marie-Gerard relationship, with Balthazar being an innocent bystander through most of it, yet being at the receiving end of unwarranted attacks and unnecessary cruelty, simply accepting his unjustified persecution.

The clearest scene to which we can point to the “caught in the middle” theme translating from the narrative to physical presence is the sequence depicting Gerard and Marie in the latter’s car, engaging in semi-abusive sexual contact. Marie jumps out of the car as she attempts to fight Gerard’s advances — here, the human drama makes its way to Balthazar. He is literally stuck in the middle of the conflict, and Bresson films the sequence to ensure that the audience is spatially aware of the donkey’s sudden, undue introduction to a predicament he should not be concerned with. Gerard chases Marie around Balthazar’s body, and Bresson cuts from time to time to show Balthazar’s unvarying face — a psychologically unmotivated model that sagaciously endures the confusing defects and corruptions of the humans around him. Rather than running away, Balthazar stands rock solid, physically standing in the middle to split the conflict into two sides. This spatial ordering of the film space concretizes Balthazar’s role as an observer who experiences undue hostility. He is a scapegoat of human indecency, simply because of convenience — he himself is often never the intended objective. The donkey is simply a means to (or resistance against) an end.

As such, Bresson often reminds the audience of Balthazar’s presence as a character both figuratively and physically stuck in the middle of human drama, unable to escape, indicating his recurring role as an avenue to, or blockade from, a human objective. We observe Balthazar filling screen space even when he seemingly has nothing to do with the content of the narrative or “personal” interest the conflict. The Marie-Gerard chase scene is the clearest instantiation but it is observable in other scenes before and later on in the film, like the Jacques-Marie conversation where the camera deliberately cuts to Balthazar occupying the screen as the former speaks about Marie’s beauty, or when the same two characters share dialogue at the end of the film, in which shots of Balthazar eating are inserted into a conversation seemingly unrelated to him. However, such shots are constant reminders of Balthazar’s lack of independent agency, often serving as an accessory to or instrument of human desire.

Thus, when we are confronted with the death scene that is stylistically similar to these previous sequences — Balthazar finding himself in the middle of other characters — but atmospherically transformed, its effect is larger, augmented by associations to previous images. Instead of standing in the middle of Gerard and Marie, used as a tool of human foible, Balthazar has found himself back in his natural habitat; joined by other animals, he has seemingly regained his agency. No one is telling him what to do, no one is torturing him, no one is forcing him into superficial human drama. The sheep seem to almost praise him, gently forming a circle around him, standing in contrast to his physical presence in the aforementioned scenes, where he is utilized as a mere means to an end, or an obstacle to overcome. Bresson cultivates a relationship between his images through Balthazar’s spatial positioning and arrangement on screen, facilitating a reading of the final scene that is nearly impossible to dismiss as unremarkable and modest. A character we have seen getting tossed around due to his unfortunate position as an adversely affected observer finally regains his own independent agency at death, in the midst of others biologically similar to him. That description in itself would push any member of the audience to accept the final scene as redemptive and graceful.

In this vein, Au Hasard Balthazar has taken advantage of the visual medium and the spatiotemporal tools at its disposal to establish relationships between images that assemble the aesthetic beauty of Balthazar’s death scene. All are typically Bressonian mechanisms. However, he also uses sound in a distinctly un-Bressonian manner to convey the same message. The director has often stated his penchant for using silence and ambient noise to facilitate greater subjectivity reading in the images he places on screen: “The eye (in general) superficial, the ear profound and inventive. A locomotive's whistle imprints on us a whole railroad station.”10 In a similar vein, he has often state his distaste for the use of music: “No music as accompaniment, support or rein­forcement. No music at all…Music takes up all the room and gives no in­creased value to the image…It isolates your film from the life of your film.”11 Bresson follows this rule almost religiously — he only includes non-diegetic music in the opening title sequences and ending credits of nearly all of his films leading to Au Hasard Balthazar. He follows this rule throughout this film as well, until surprisingly breaking it in Balthazar’s death scene. In many preceding scenes, we have seen that perceptual framing, ellipses, and spatial arrangements of characters and shots are what creates an internalized sensation of struggle for the audience, which experiences catharsis in the final scene. Emotions are never overt, hidden behind silence and implications of image relationships for us to find. However, this principle goes out the window the second Schubert’s Piano Sonata No. 20, Andantino is overlayed on top of a Balthazar that restfully takes his final breaths.

It is “a powerful modifier, like alcohol or dope,” as Bresson labels it, and as such, without it, the impact of Balthazar’s death would undoubtedly be subdued.12 There would most likely be greater debate about the scene’s interpretation if the noises only came from the bleating sheep and the occasional rustling of the grass. Silence would open up greater psychological dialogue in the audience’s mind as to how to emotionally understand Balthazar’s death. Bresson consciously chooses to suppress music in his other films and even earlier in Au Hasard Balthazar, using “silence as an element of composition and means to emotion.”13 It grounds films into reality, presenting the circumstances as purely factual. Yet, Schubert’s sonata acts as “destroyer of the real,” and exaggerates the emotional status of the death scene.14 A stark departure from typical Bressonian cinematic tools, the audience is pushed towards feeling a certain emotion rather than figuring it out for themselves. Thus, addition of sublime music incontrovertibly embellishes the scene with beautiful aesthetic characteristics, and Balthazar’s death is consequently an even more polished piece of emotional beauty that separates itself from the rest of the film.

It is simply inconceivable to state that Balthazar’s death is a basic presentation of an old animal’s demise. The contrasts in visual presentation and imagery — especially of animal characters — communicate variances in character emotion and mood that are too great to ignore; the spatiotemporal information that is inserted and omitted facilitates the association of conflicting themes like immorality and sanctity that inevitably return in its undeniable relationship to the final scene; the addition of unexpected musical qualities, especially for a director who has so strongly objected to conveying messages through music, injects aesthetic qualities into the final moments of the film that ultimately embellish the points of the cinematographic tools that Bresson maneuvers. The death scene is a catharsis — an animal’s freedom from physical shackles that he shared with other animals in the film, the spiritual release of a stoic saint that has carried the pain for and provided comfort to the family that cared for him, the restoration of a Balthazar’s status as a liberated agent after being treated as an intermediary force to sustain human pursuit, and most of all, a peaceful, beautiful end to a tortured soul who has endured more pain than any other character in the film. I will continue viewing the scene as beautiful; it is impossible for me to do otherwise.
