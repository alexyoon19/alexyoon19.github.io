Facing Death, Alone or Together

Philosophical Schism: Heidegger and Hegel’s Competing Visions of Full Authentic Existence in the Face of Death

Essay for Robert Pippin’s seminar on Heidegger’s Being and Time, University of Chicago. March 2023.

Heidegger embarks on a journey in Being and Time, especially in Division II, to expound the conditions for the unity of the authentic self, a perspective that captures the authentic existence of Dasein in it wholeness. Division I, even in all its talks about primordiality and care, possesses an inadequacy due to “the hermeneutical Situation from which the preceding analysis has arisen.” Particularly, all of the ontological elements within which we have conducted the analytic of Dasein thus far has “been confined to the analysis of such existing as is either undifferentiated or inauthentic.”1 We have yet to talk about Dasein in a wholly authentic manner, since our analytic has been broadened to delineating “the possibility of having any understanding of Being at all,” and have not understood how and why such intelligibility is essential for Dasein—the entity to which Being at all is meaningful.2 Thus, in establishing the primordial foundations for how Dasein can open itself to “the possibility of authentic existence,” Heidegger appeals to the essential temporality through which Dasein can articulate the meaning of Being at all: death.3 Death is what facilitates a consideration of Dasein in its authentic wholeness. In detailing an authentic Being-towards-death, Heidegger calls forth conceptions of anticipation in the face of such inevitable and insurmountable death, how anxiety results from this disclosure, and how resoluteness can orient Dasein to wholly commit to itself in an ownmost manner while facing a groundless existence.

However, in formulating such an abstracted theory of the meaning of Being, Heidegger fails to articulate how we should act in light of his existential analytic. Heidegger states that we must act in an “anticipatorily resolute” manner in the face of temporality and death to achieve a self-unity that is both authentic and essential to the meaning of Being. Yet, in the face of our absurd thrownness and complex inherited history, Heidegger seems to be largely silent on the issue of how such a practice of resolutely committing and ultimately acting to realize an utmost authentic individuality in the face of the “they” looks like. How do we actually “achieve” being resolute in the world, if it is a possible “achievement” at all? Thus, it remains to be seen whether Heidegger’s account of authentic existence can constitute “a wholeness.” In attempting to escape the indeterminateness presented by Heidegger in Division II of Being and Time, we may turn to Hegel’s Phenomenology of Spirit for potential clarification, specifically in his theories of activity and self-transformative conception of Geist. As such, to elucidate greater insight into the “meaning of Being” from a dialogue between these German philosophers, I will first annunciate the particulars of Heidegger’s attempt to establish Dasein’s authentic existence. Subsequently, I will explain how Hegel presents a comprehensive challenge to Heidegger’s rejection of absorption in the they (das Man), given our inheritances and future possibilities. I will end by considering a potential Heideggerian response to the Hegelian position and critique, one that grounds itself in resoluteness and historicity.

As Being-in-the-world, Dasein comports itself to a multitude of possibilities, essentially being “ahead-of-itself” at any given moment, opening itself up towards specific modes of existence. Such “ahead-of-itself” is an “item in the structure of care, [telling] us unambiguously that in Dasein there is always something still outstanding…it is essential to the basic constitution of Dasein that there is constantly something still to be settled.”4 In this respect, Dasein is always available to and immersed in the world; the primacy of my being is dependent on how I commit myself and am understood in some concentrated fashion. Nevertheless, such an openness to possibilities means that Dasein is incomplete, a lack of total coherence. We have not considered yet how Dasein as Being-in-the-world, can reach a whole in light of this openness—how Dasein fulfills itself.

In such consideration, Heidegger reaches the determination that Dasein experiences its existential completion in its utterly personal relation to ontological death, in which its character is precisely understood. We have already established in Division I that Dasein “comports itself towards its Being as its own most possibility. In each case Dasein is its possibility.”5 Thus, death must also be a particular mode of possibility, if it is to complete Dasein. In this respect, Heidegger first establishes death as “the possibility of no-longer-being-able-to-be-there…all its relations to any other Dasein have been undone. This ownmost non-relational possibility is at the same time the uttermost one…Dasein cannot outstrip the possibility of death.”6 Yet, this definition is incredibly abstract—it seems like a jumbled collection of philosophical words at first glance. It is further confounded by the fact that we consider the concept of death through a multitude of lenses: biological, psychological, environmental, social, and historical (this list is not comprehensive). Thus, it is unclear how these conceptions relate to Dasein’s existential death, making the establishment of Dasein’s death as an ontological conception an incredibly complex task. Nevertheless, we can inch closer to understanding how ontological death brings us closer to authentic existence through first considering what ontological death is not.

We know that Dasein is, as Being-in-the-world, fundamentally absorbed within the world. Thus, the existence of the ontical phenomena that we study, such as biology, psychology, and sociology is predicated upon the ontology of Dasein, “which is superordinate to an ontology of life.”7 The disciplines above only exist because they represent a possible way of Being-in-the-world—the saliency of these specific activities arise from Dasein’s relationship-building with the rest of the world, founded upon Being-in-the-world. As such, it is clear that we cannot conclude the existential notion of death to be one that we are immediately familiar with, since these are already established upon a preliminary, existential conception of death that is associated with Dasein. Whether it be biological death, what Heidegger designates as perishing, or any other ontical death, what Heidegger designates as demise, does not fully capture the termination of Dasein as an existential Being-in-the-world. These alternate conceptions of death are seen as events that happen, but as we have established, Dasein’s death is not an event—it is a possibility.

Ontological death carries a distinct signature for Dasein. It cannot just be any possibility, since it is the “uttermost,” the ultimate possibility, as labeled above. Specifically, death fundamentally sculpts every other possibility of Dasein—the very “phenomenon of care” that is constitutive of Dasein’s Being.8 Such maximal significance of death may not be immediately salient or explicit, but is evident in considering our everyday contention with death. Often, our culture (and many others around the world) will tell us “not to fear death,” or “not to worry about death.” The valorization of collective indifference towards death induces us to rationalize death as a common eventuality, “understood as an indefinite something which, above all, must duly arrive from somewhere or other, but which is proximally not yet present-at-hand for oneself, and is therefore no threat.”9 In fleeing from death, Dasein seeks false consolation in the “they” that “gives its approval, and aggravates the temptation to cover up from oneself one’s ownmost Being-towards-death. This evasive concealment in the face of death dominates everydayness so stubbornly.”10 But in classic Heideggerian fashion, such escape of confronting death is simply another ontical mode of Being-towards-death. Fleeing, evading, seeking consolation—all this strategizing simply are ways in which we come to grips with death, constantly orienting ourselves to different approaches to take “in the face of death.”11 This hidden possibility permeates the deepest levels of Dasein, ever-present, carrying the “definite character of Being-towards-death, even when it is not explicitly engaged in ‘thinking about death.’… its concern is merely in the mode of an untroubled indifference towards the uttermost possibility of existence.”12

In this respect, Dasein is essentially connected with death, yet, is for the most part always evading or otherwise concealing itself from this unsurpassable, certain threat of death. After all, thinking about death is exhausting. It is easier not to confront it. It may not even be wrong to avoid directly grappling with it at every moment. As such, we live in a mode of constant distraction, which characterizes a “kind of Being into which Dasein can divert itself and has for the most part always diverted itself.”13 Thus, to end the analytic of death here would be to have an incomplete ontological conception of it—the fact that Dasein is not directly confronting death, evading its ownmost, non-relational, and un-outstrip-able possibility, suggests a state in which it is actually owning this possibility.

Indeed, Heidegger proposes that this “falling evasion in the face of death is an inauthentic Being-towards-death.”14 If Dasein’s Being is always at issue for itself, it seems that an authentic Being-towards-death is one in which the individual Dasein is in fact directly owning this ownmost possibility, not in subjection to others: “Authentic Being-towards-death can not evade its ownmost non-relational possibility, or cover up this possibility by thus fleeing from it, or give a new explanation for it to accord with the common sense of the “they”…we must set forth those items in such a Being which are constitutive for it as an understanding of death.”15 If we are to comport ourselves authentically towards our uttermost possibility of impossibility, we cannot be seeking consolation in the “they”—das Man. Death can no longer be mediated by or filtered through the lens of others, because it has to be ownmost and non-relational. Das Man has no normative authority in telling me what death actually is, because death is the possibility of the absolute impossibility of my being, not anyone else’s. Thus, by understanding that das Man will ultimately fail in making the possibility of death fully salient to us, that “Being-with Others, will fail us when our ownmost potentiality-for-being is the issue,” is the only way we can authentically be ourselves.16 To be clear, this understanding is not just a contemplation of my death, but rather, understanding in Heidegger’s sense: a potentiality-for-Being that Dasein can project itself onto, giving rise to the significance of situations, actions, and interpretations.

But how does Dasein make such an understanding of the possibility of death “possible for itself of its own accord,” such that “death…individualizes Dasein down to itself”?17 Heidegger clearly states that we do not do achieve this understanding by brooding on it, and he is certainly not asking us to actualize death in the way we do with other possibilities. Thus, authentic Being-towards-death is to recognize and respond to the fact that it is ever-impending, unsurpassable, and certain—to be in “anticipation of this possibility,” rather than expecting death as an event that happens or finding false solace in das Man.18 Anticipation of finitude becomes the condition by which Dasein finds significance in other possibilities. A vulnerability towards the impending impossibility of existence percolates through Dasein’s existence, and we must come to understand that without the filtration of the “they.” It is the barest form of existential projecting: a self-understanding without any existentiell, mediating possibilities to understand ourselves in terms of. As death is simultaneously indefinite yet inevitable, such acceptance lets us become “free for one’s own death, one is liberated from one’s lostness in those possibilities which may accidentally thrust themselves upon one…for the first time one can authentically understand and choose among the factical possibilities lying ahead of that possibility.”19 Instead of fleeing, Dasein now runs forth towards death, rejecting the mindless conformity in das Man, accepting the conception as my uttermost existential possibility.

Through such anticipation, “the possibility of authentic existence…of understanding one’s ownmost and uttermost potentiality-for-Being” arises.20 In looking forward and confronting the essential finitude of its own existence, Dasein considers itself in a wholeness that was not founded in das Man before due to the uttermost quality of death: “In anticipation Dasein can first make certain of its ownmost Being in its totality.”21 But Heidegger has yet to explain how this authentic Being-towards-death discloses itself to Dasein. We have an anticipatory understanding of death, but how does it project itself upon Dasein? After all, Heidegger establishes that “all understanding is accompanied by a state-of-mind.”22 Thus, in anticipation, Dasein owns up to its potentiality-for-Being in death, but at the same time, “Dasein opens itself to a constant threat arising out of its own “there”.”23 The resulting state-of-mind from the anticipatory understanding of death as a uttermost potentiality-for-Being is one that Heidegger labels anxiety.24 In this state, Dasein finds itself in a vacuum; in its thrownness, the previously “disclosed world” with all its familiar significance is ripped away from Dasein. We find ourselves “face to face with the “nothing” of the possible impossibility of its existence,” or death.25 Thus, anxiety is not fear of a particular object, but best described as being

“anxious about…Being-in-the-world itself. In anxiety what is environmentally ready-to-hand sinks away, and so, in general, do entities within-the-world. The ‘world’ can offer nothing more, and neither can the Dasein-with of Others. Anxiety thus takes away from Dasein the possibility of understanding itself, as it falls….Anxiety throws Dasein back upon that which it is anxious about—its authentic potentiality-for-Being-in-the-world. [Thus,] anxiety individualizes Dasein for its ownmost Being-in-the-world…Anxiety makes manifest in Dasein…its Being-free for the freedom of choosing itself and taking hold of itself…the authenticity of its Being.”26

In this respect, the individuated self-understanding of Dasein on the basis of anticipation essentially induces a state of anxiety. Nothing—especially not das Man—can replace this fragility. Dasein cannot keep itself lost in the social context and the inherited norms of its time, because its Being is at issue for itself, a personal singular purposiveness. If authentic Being-towards-death is made possible through anticipation’s revealing of Dasein’s “lostness in the they-self, and brings it face to face with the possibility of being itself…in an impassioned freedom towards death,” anxiety necessarily accompanies that whole, authentic existence.27

In light of this existential analytic of death, we may be able to intellectually grasp how we inch towards to the possibility of authentic existence in its wholeness. Radical finitude, when taken in direct confrontation such that it opens the possibility of death up to Dasein in an “ownmost and non-relational” way, is the horizon through which we exist as wholly individuated, away from das Man. Nevertheless, in this authentic Being-towards-death, Dasein is only left with an indeterminacy that rejects anything dependent on das Man in its totality. Specifically, Heidegger often presents his metaphysical view of the world in what it is not. Whether it be Division I, with Being-in-the-world and knowledge, or Division II with Being-towards-death and authenticity, concepts are established with negation: anti-Cartesianism, inauthenticity, anti-conformism, anti-ontical, anti-mindlessness. These repudiations are concrete. Yet, in such rejections, there is not much of a normative, concrete existential theory of what Dasein should actually be doing. Namely, if we are not satisfied with ontical notions of death, conformism to das Man, nor with concealing death (as in, these are phenomena that we should reject as authentic existence), and anxiety permeates this authentic state, what existing structure is left for us to latch onto, given Dasein exists as Being-in-the-world? Even if we accept Heidegger’s notion of anticipation and anxiety, it seems to be empty in the positive resources that can point us towards an orientation of Dasein that does not end in this everlasting existential struggle between the ownmost individual and the entire social fabric it fundamentally is thrown into.

In facing such indeterminate lack of meaningful structures that let Dasein act in a context-ridden, historically-inherited world, we may turn to Hegel to shed new light on potentially escaping this struggle and establishing a realized wholeness. In encapsulating Hegel’s Phenomenology of Spirit in the confines of a short essay, I am very much running the risk of oversimplifying, overemphasizing, and under-appreciating the incredible depth of Hegel’s project. Nevertheless, there are core similarities between Heidegger and Hegel’s accounts of human existence that contribute to each’s respective understanding of the self. In a Heideggerian vein, Hegel states in the preface to the Phenomenology that “Death…is the most fearful thing of all…However, the life of spirit is not a life that is fearing death and austerely saving itself from ruin; rather, it bears death calmly, and in death, it sustains itself. Spirit only wins its truth by finding its feet in its absolute disruption.”28 Such a statement brings us to the near identical point of issue in Heidegger, namely, that an authentic understanding of death makes Dasein “free for one’s own death…liberated from one’s lostness in those possibilities which may accidentally thrust themselves upon one…for the first time one can authentically understand and choose among the factical possibilities lying ahead of that possibility.”29 As such, on this commonality between the German philosophers, the question remains as to how Hegel sheds more light on how we should reflect on and act in light of our essentially thrown historical reality—Being-in-the-world for Heidegger, and “a self-consciousness…for a self-consciousness…unity of itself in its otherness” for Hegel.30

Hegel recognizes the tension between the individual and the rest of the world in truthfully actualizing a self-consciousness—the same issue Heidegger tackles when dealing with authentic existence. Hegel first states that the societal customs and “laws express what each individual is and does. The singular individual takes cognizance of them as not only his universal objective thinghood, but rather as himself within them…and in each of his fellow citizens.”31 Yet, only in principle is reason actualized in the life of a free people; it cannot last, since a free people are not defined only by their commonalities. We all possess our own individuality, striving towards distinct means as self-consciousnesses. Thus, I, as a singular spirit, must first make myself an individual in the world, asserting my individuality by way of practical effort, through which other individual self-consciousnesses can identify me as. In this respect, I fulfill my own will, not the will and customs of the social whole, and my “purpose therefore is to give [my]self actualization as a singular individual and, as such, to take please in giving [my]self [my] actualization.”32 However, at the same time, I cannot be realized in isolation, as we have to “intuit [my] immediate abstract being-for-itself, or to intuit [myself] in another self-consciousness,” where I can see myself as reflected.33

At this point, we see the inklings of Hegel’s break with Heidegger. Both agree on an anti-Cartesian view of reality, and the fact that the individual can find his self-sustenance in accepting “absolute disruption” of itself (death). However, it is clear that Hegel believes that the collective plays a role in reifying the self-consciousness, while Heidegger claims that in the face of anxiety, the inherited world “can offer nothing more, and neither can the Dasein-with of Others.”34 The Hegelian perspective goes a step further, and while establishing that an individuality cannot be completely rooted in commonality, there is a reason why the social practices I find myself thrown into has some authority over me, and why some of them are sufficient or insufficient in my “actualization as a singular individual.”35 What Heidegger fails to explain in death, anticipation, and anxiety is how Dasein should proceed, given these difficulties; he wraps all of these timely social norms, practices, and traditions into an abstract conception of das Man and negates its entirety. Apparently, we can only find “an impassioned freedom towards death” in a wholly individuated, anxious vacuum, when “it projects itself upon its ownmost potentiality-for-Being rather than upon the possibility of the they-self.”36 On the other hand, Hegel speaks about the Romans, Greeks, the Enlightenment, and the Romantics in explaining why historical, social movements are dynamic, self-transformative authorities based in free, collective recognition and affirmation of institutions by rational agents that produce enduring cultural products—literature, art, drama, music, and so on.

Thus, Heidegger presents an account of how through death, the insufficiency of sociality, why we cannot find authentic existence in the thrownness of the “they.” There is a constant rejection of why the ideas and authority of das Man do not work in establishing a whole individual and that there is inherent self-contingency and unique finitude of any “grounding” that Dasein undertakes. Attempts to contend with the Others’ definitions, especially about death, is concealment, fleeing, evasion, inauthentic. However, we must ask if accepting any practice that das Man possesses truly is a complete submission to the “they”—is it really a mindless, non-reflective conformism to an external power that provides nothing towards authentic existence? Is there no way in which normative, consciously reflective considerations of Shakespeare’s plays, Beethoven’s symphonies, Dante’s epics, Bresson’s films, or Aristotle’s philosophy sustains an essential, necessary, constitutive part of our Being, rather than an anxious Dasein in an “evasive concealment in the face of death”?37 Thus, it seems this account cannot be a satisfactory unified theory of self, if we are not presented with the backdrop on which the complete failure of das Man occurs and the rational standard of failure on which a total personal rejection of such enduring normative social models is necessary. Thus, Hegel’s perspective that historical progression is an essential part of the self’s actualization, that:

“The aim, absolute knowing, or spirit knowing itself as spirit, has its path in the recollection of spirits as they are in themselves and are as they achieve the organization of their realm. Their preservation according to their free-standing existence appearing in the form of contingency is history, but according to their conceptually grasped organization, it is the science of phenomenal knowing. Both together are conceptually grasped history; they form the recollection and the Golgotha of absolute spirit, the actuality, the truth, the certainty of its throne, without which it would be lifeless and alone; only – out of the chalice of this realm of spirits, foams forth to him his infinity.”38

seems to present a concrete challenge to Heidegger’s claim of the possibility of whole authentic existence arises only out of a complete return to the self through anticipatory confrontation of death, as it lacks a constructive explanation of preserved history and thus cannot constitute a whole.

It would be intellectually dishonest to not mention Heidegger’s attempts to show how the individual returns to itself through an anticipatory understanding of death and the subsequent state-of-mind that arises after anxiety. He mentions concepts of resoluteness and historicality that pose a potential circumvention to a Hegelian’s perspective — namely, how a Hegelian perspective does not get to the primordial question of what the “meaning of Being in general” actually is.39 Such an appeal to a Hegelian self-transformative, rational self-consciousness is ultimately a phenomenal assumption grounded upon a substantialist account of human existence. In simple terms, why does Hegel’s conception of rational, self-transforming sociality matter to us at all? In the Heideggerian perspective, Hegel’s philosophy of history is simply a complete yet ontical possibility of meaningfulness predicated upon a more primordial existentialist structure, rather than an answer to the primordial question of the meaning of Being in general. Shakespeare’s plays, Beethoven’s symphonies, Dante’s epics, and Aristotle’s philosophy can only come to potentially matter to an individual through a

“Resoluteness in which Dasein comes back to itself, discloses current factical possibilities of authentic existing, and discloses them in terms of the heritage which that resoluteness, as thrown, takes over. In one’s coming back resolutely to one’s thrownness, there is hidden a handing down to oneself of the possibilities that have come down to one…the handing down of a heritage constitutes itself in resoluteness…once one has grasped the finitude of one’s existence, it snatches one back rom the endless multiplicity of possibilities which offer themselves as the closest one…This is how we designate Dasein’s primordial historizing…in which Dasein hands itself down to itself, free for death, in a possibility which it has inherited and yet has chosen.”40

Thus, it seems Heidegger would say to Hegel that a rejection of certain historical norms and rationalization of others is only possible because of a belief (or even assumption) that there exists a deeper process of “choosing” inherited possibilities for Dasein, such that the contingency of what matters depends on Dasein and its finitude (death). An appeal to Hegelian Geist is thus simply another attempt to evade the ultimacy of death, to find solace in some socialized notion of mattering and meaning of Being. In this respect, to Heidegger, Hegel’s rational, self-transformative conception of history cannot be a primordial source of wholeness and meaningfulness for Dasein, since “it can have something of the sort because the Being of this entity is constituted by historicality,” namely, an ontological meaningfulness of historizing “in which Dasein is stretched along and stretches itself along…disclosing and interpreting belong essentially to Dasein’s historizing.”41

Short of resurrecting our two German philosophers discussed, there seems to be no way to reach a satisfactory conclusion to objections that both bring to each other. Endless volumes can be written on the contention between these figures; this essay has barely begun to unravel the dialogue that can take place. I must also concede that I have not fully explored the ontological conception of resoluteness and historicality, given the limitations of a short essay. Yet, even in the face of historicality, Heidegger still does not seem to have a satisfactory, rational account of normative authority that informs Dasein on how to actualize its authentic existence in the world. Hegel might say that his realization of Geist is the necessary historical success that counters the failures of das Man, since “Spirit only wins its truth by finding its feet in its absolute disruption.”42 Thus, Hegel’s Phenomenology may concretely ascribe to reality and mastermind the very manifestation of a free-for-death choosing process that Heidegger discusses—a non-theoretical discussion of “the possibility that Dasein may choose its hero.”43 Accordingly, to some Hegelians, Heideggerian discussions of death, anticipation, anxiety, resoluteness, and historicality in establishing on authentic existence leaves us with a depressingly empty, radically intellectualized account of how and why the rational purposive agent consciously latches on to certain aspects of the social fabric—that Dasein as a “null basis of its own nullity” presents a hollow, incomplete conception of human existence.44 But Being and Time as a standalone book is unfinished. Perhaps all we can do in light of his half-done project is to speculate, drawing on figures like Hegel and competing concepts of self-consciousness and human existence to “MacGyver” the loose ends present. Maybe there is no answer at all, and Heidegger is largely correct in that we are trying to rationalize an utterly empty void that somehow holds up a fragile, uncanny existence, thus fleeing from our fundamental “meaning of Being” as “permeated with nullity through and through.”45
