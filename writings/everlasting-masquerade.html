Nietzsche’s Masks

An Everlasting Masquerade: The Implications of Nietzschean Esotericism and Solitude

Essay for a seminar with Robert Pippin, University of Chicago. May 2023.

It is quite difficult to understand what Nietzsche specifically aims at in any of his texts; even in what he claims to be the most accessible and thorough account of his views, Beyond Good and Evil is saturated with talks of voyages, of monstrous and terrifying masks, of magnificent tensions of spirit, of silence and concealment, of a dangerous Perhaps. Yet, it may be that Nietzsche considered the book as his most thorough, exactly for the reasons we consider it confusing and somewhat stupefying. If his book is meant to be a true prelude—in the way that Wagner or Tchaikovsky’s preludes and overtures do not simply thematically explain, but themselves embody and express some operatic or literary theme in the musical language—it is conceivable, even probable, that Nietzsche himself includes these terrifying masks, ambiguous silences, dangerous perhapses, and layered intentions in the text itself. Even the two somewhat crystallized philosophical claims he makes—the will to power and perspectivism—are shrouded in tension, at times proclaimed with absolute courage, only to fall back onto a self-aware tentativeness and wry concealment. Thus, in discussing Beyond Good and Evil and the philosophical implications Nietzsche brings to the table, the consideration of masks is essential. In the face of this esotericism that he speaks of throughout the book and employs in the text itself, we may begin to understand what the notions of perspectivism and the will to power might constitute, insofar as they are not simply relativistic theories of metaphysics or epistemology, dressed up in ornamental language to make it sound nice.

Specifically, the passage in §289 not only echoes what he has said in the previous 288 sections on esotericism and masks, but also imparts to the reader, a final, sustained reminder about Nietzsche’s own words and his anticipation of the philosophy of the future: “Every philosophy is a foreground philosophy — that is a hermit’s judgement: “There is something arbitrary in his stopping here, looking back, looking around, in his not digging any deeper here, and putting his spade away — there is also something suspicious about it.” Every philosophy conceals a philosophy too: every opinion is also a hiding place, every word is also a mask.”1 However, we should not take by these words that Nietzsche simply means there is a deeper meaning behind Kant or Spinoza or Rousseau’s output that we have to dig into and eventually find. Namely, this paragraph seems to hearken back all the way to the preface, where he states that “all great things, in order to inscribe eternal demands in the heart of humanity, must first wander the earth under monstrous and terrifying masks; dogmatic philosophy was this sort of mask.”2

There must be a reason, however concealed or silently said, that Nietzsche continues to nudge us towards the view that his vision of a new philosophy is necessarily an esoteric enterprise. On this account, it seems that new philosophy constitutes at least two things: it “demands” that we commit to understanding such masks, but with the constant reminder that a single identification of a mask may be a diversion from another, more deeply hidden mask (whether involuntarily or voluntarily worn), and thus, it is also an eternal unearthing exercise, a reminder to avoid establishing finalities, attempting rather to formulate an anthology of the multiplicative interpretability of claims, of philosophies, of philosophers (like Nietzsche himself), and perhaps most importantly, of ourselves. I will first devote my attention to the initial part of why and how Nietzsche demands us to devote our evaluation towards the necessity of masks and the scrutiny of such esotericism, even if the content may be distractions. Subsequently, I will consider what these demands and esoteric conditions say about the enterprise of future philosophy and what meaning it may impart on Nietzsche’s own ideas on perspectivism and the will to power.

Solitude seems to play a major role in the necessity of masks and concealment for the “choice human being” that the “new breed of philosophers” will constitute. Not only does Nietzsche describe “higher men” first as “hermits,” but throughout the book, he hints at the fact that these hermits are immersed in, perhaps even want and welcome, the isolation that the general public shun.3 People that are not “of a higher taste” certainly are comfortable with living in a mode of distraction, possessing an autophobia that drives them instead towards the external stimulus of crowds, of social media, of loud and boisterous parties. However, “every choice human being strives instinctively for a citadel and secrecy where he is rescued from the crowds, the many, the vast majority; where, as the exception, he can forget the human norm.”4 From this passage taken out of context, such a pursuit seems not too far from the projects of a Kant or a Newton, who forged their revolutionary theories of knowledge and nature in complete isolation, rejecting the calls of “well-wishers and friends” to bring them out of loneliness.5

Yet, this reading is immediately complicated by Nietzsche proclaiming that those who are detached from crowds, “placid and proud and hidden in his citadel,” are “not made for knowledge, not predestined for it,” which suggests that the hermit’s secrecy and citadel are not simply flights from reality in pursuit of academic projects, a mere self-confinement to an intellectual ivory tower.6 But also, the “philosopher was always a hermit first,” perhaps as Kant and Newton were.7 Thus, what is it about this Nietzschean hermit that it stretches beyond our conventional definition of the word, that every philosopher was one at some point, but that a hermit’s writings also exhibit “a new and more dangerous type of silence, of concealment”?8

We may get a better view of this secrecy that hermits and profound thinkers possess, by understanding the “terrifying mask” of Plato, who Nietzsche (without explanation) considers “the most beautiful outgrowth of antiquity.”9 Specifically, Nietzsche speaks of Plato’s secrecy peeking out from behind its mask due to a “happily preserved petit fait (little fact)”: that “under the pillow of his deathbed they did not find a “Bible” or anything Egyptian, Pythagorean, or Platonic — but instead, Aristophanes.”10 We are quick to assign Platonism to what it exhibits on the surface, namely, the theory of forms, the doctrine of the immortal soul, and the epistemological principles he outlines in the Phaedo and the Republic. Yet, the ethos of the Platonic project seems to shift dramatically when we consider that Plato could not have “endured life — a Greek life that he said No to — without an Aristophanes!”11 Could it be that Plato’s foundational philosophy—all this serious talk of properties, forms, truth values, and abstract objects—silently begs a deeper “subtlety of interpretation”?12 Could it be that Plato “used knowledge merely as a tool” to accomplish other tasks or to express other messages, that the virtue of the academy and the symposium was, in part, a mask for an “involuntary confession,” one that expresses a “philosophical sense of humor…artistic curiosity…a long and secret labor” for the comics, the slanderers, the clever poets, the satirists, and the tragedians?13 Are the critiques of poetry in the Republic and the critiques of comedy in the Philebus masquerades that divert from hidden commentaries and desires?

And so, philosophy is a riddling mask in this sense; a project whose “intention is only a sign and symptom that first needs to be interpreted, and that moreover, it is a sign that means too many things and consequently means almost nothing by itself.”14 Thus, while at times lamenting the path that Platonism and Christianity has set for Europe, Nietzsche considers Plato “the most beautiful outgrowth of antiquity” because of the seeming deeper motivations that lie behind Plato’s “masks,” as evinced by his apparent dying appreciation for Aristophanes. The existence of Platonism is thus a “surface and skin — which, like every skin, reveals something but conceals even more.”15 The depth of Plato is realized in attempting to understand why he wanted the plays of a comic and a cynic near him as he took his final breaths, rather than the serious, wise words of a Pythagoras or Socrates. Given that Platonism has been taken in its surface as dogmatic, inducing Europe to talk about “the spirit and the Good” for centuries, it seems all the more mysterious and whimsical that the virtuous Plato gave his last thoughts to a clever rhetorician like Aristophanes. Nietzsche gives us an insight into why that might be: “everything profound loves masks; the most profound things go so far as to hate images and likenesses. Wouldn’t just the opposite be a proper disguise for the shame of a god?”16 We remember with the mention of “god” here that Nietzsche thinks Christianity is Platonism “for the people.”17 Thus, Platonism’s strongest convictions are perhaps “echo[es] of the desert,” masks and riddles that conceal “the most beautiful outgrowth of antiquity” and transform it into entities unrecognizable, exactly because it seeks to obscure “the shame of a god.”18

The solitude of our Nietzschean hermits and philosophers, which include the “Heraclitean, Platonic, Empedoclean types”, do not come from the physical self-confinement to an isolated project of intellectual virtue, as Kant or Schopenhauer would advocate for. It seems to be a sort of perplexing, at times self-deceptive art—after all, it is inconceivable that Plato would not recognize the incongruence in writing that “the ridiculous is in its main aspect a kind of vice,” or that “no composer of comedy, iambic or lyric verse shall be permitted to hold any citizen up to laughter,” only to lay in his deathbed with lyrics from his friend Aristophanes, “the Prince of Ancient Comedy.”19 As such, the solitude is found in the esotericism itself, the “doing everything I can to be hard to understand myself.” Plato seems to have covered up his “acts of love and extravagant generosity” by taking “a stick and giv[ing] the eye-witnesses a good beating: this will obscure any memory traces.”20 The philosopher’s esotericism is the citadel that guards his profound shame; he cannot be understood by the ‘lower souls and lowlier life-forces,” the “petty people” who would exclaim things like “to hell with good taste! The norm is more interesting than the exception — than me, the exception!”21 The secrecy lies in the interpretive nature of the writing, where we encounter the profound complexities, the inexplicable internal dissonance, and the questionable questions that are raised in considering hidden meanings, like the meaning behind Plato’s relationship with Aristophanes, and what that means for the former’s own work.

In this affirmation of masked, esoteric writing, Nietzsche hints at a decisively anti-modern, anti-Enlightenment ideal: that there are indeed lower and higher souls, a belief in “an order of rank and not in equality and equal rights.”22 The exoteric cannot grasp what the esoteric says, because:

the exoteric sees things up from below — while the esoteric sees them down from above! There are heights of the soul from whose vantage point even tragedy stops having tragic effect… What helps feed or nourish he higher type of man must be almost poisonous to a very different and lesser type. The virtues of a base man could indicate vices and weaknesses in a philosopher…There are books that have inverse values for soul and for health, depending on whether they are used by the lower souls and lowlier life-forces, or by the higher and more powerful ones…Books for the general public always smell foul.23

Such words comment on the nature of perspectivism itself. For example, certain readings of Nietzsche’s own oeuvre, result in a Leopold and Loeb or antisemitic National Socialism, while readings by other types of souls, result in an Edvard Munch, a Gustav Mahler, or a Thomas Mann. These figures were all influenced by Nietzsche’s words, yet in the former case, many would comment that they were “dangerous and cause[d] deterioration and dissolution,” while the latter cases seem to have resulted in “the most subtle, genuinely honest, and also the most malicious consciences of the day, who are living touchstones of the soul.”24 And so, hermits, which Nietzsche considers himself to be one throughout the book (i.e. “we hermits and marmots”25), stay steeped in solitude, not from self-imposed projects of knowledge, but from the differences in perspective. He is isolated, because those lower souls interpret his words as “stupidities, or possibly crimes,” while the higher souls toil away to chip away at his speech that is “silent and concealed…evading communication…to wander around, in his place, through the hearts and heads of his friends.”26

To us, the usage of the words “mask” or “secrecy” or “citadel” may suggest that there is some authentic face, naked truth, or hidden town that we could perhaps unmask. Perhaps by understanding why Plato was enamored with Aristophanes, we could seek and decisively single out an interpretation of Platonism that “wins out.” After all, as undergraduate philosophers, we are always asked to assess arguments, to add interlocutors and counterclaims, to assert a claim and defend it towards a conclusion. But Nietzsche comes barreling through again, telling us that there is not a single riddle that must be solved, but that “a mask is constantly growing around every profound spirit, thanks to the consistently false (which into say, shallow) interpretation of every word, every step, every sign of life he displays.”27 Thus, as much as a profound thinker creates his own masks, we seem to participate in the addition of even more. We risk exponentiating the hermit’s solitude through the very interpretations of the masks we seek to understand them by. So we ask: did Plato really love Aristophanes’ comedies that much? What if it was all just sarcasm, a puzzling experiment for his audience to consider? Maybe both? Neither?

In this respect, Nietzsche seems to hint that the uncovering of masks and decoding of riddles is an inexhaustible, but still indispensable part of the philosophy of the future. Any one act of unmasking is simply an avenue into other masks—but this is part of its design and not a bug. We discover new things, continuously digging with no single objective, using the author as a bridge (as in Thus Spoke Zarathustra, mankind is “a dangerous crossing, a dangerous on-the-way, a dangerous looking back, a dangerous shuddering and standing still”28) into a new, deeper insight, a new involuntary confession, reaching a more bizarre mask just when you think you’ve reached the end of the parade of riddles. It is a futile objective to try establishing finalities about these philosophers, because “it is typical of them to want to remain riddles in some respect.”29 He crystallizes these cryptic messages about esoteric philosophy a bit further in §289, establishing that:

The hermit does not believe that a philosopher — given that a philosopher was always a hermit first — has ever expressed his actual and final opinions in books: don’t people write books precisely to keep what they hide to themselves? In fact, he will doubt whether a philosopher could even have ‘final and actual’ opinions, whether for a philosopher every cave does not have, must not have, an even deeper cave behind it – a more extensive, stranger, richer world above the surface, an abyss behind every ground, under every ‘groundwork.’30

Thus, our new philosophers, unlike the philosophers past, will be shrouded in tentativeness, a sensitivity that their judgments of words cannot be the decisive evaluation. As such, Nietzsche describes these new philosophers “as those who attempt.” We attempt to understand Plato and his many faces but resist the urge to assign any single interpretation with finality (another question we may ask Plato is why he disliked art). And again, in a twist of self-awareness, Nietzsche ends his thoughts by turning the words on himself, reminding the reader that his own words are “only an attempt” in themselves, a mere “temptation.”31

It would be appropriate question here, if Nietzsche is asking us to engage in a pointless enterprise. Namely, is the philosophy of the future just meant to be an inherently feeble project? Are we no longer supposed to make strong assertions and defend them due to the lack of finality, continuously self-undermining the evaluations we do make because of the endless caves that lie behind endless caves? Such a project certainly seems to counter Nietzsche own life’s work, one that sought to warn us of the coming breakdown of conviction in the Enlightenment ethos and somehow replace it. In this respect, a feeble indeterminacy seems not what Nietzsche wants us to pursue—there is certainly no shortage of boldness or poise in the stylistic presentation of his words throughout the book. To better understand what he wants future philosophers to do, it would be beneficial for us to take a step back and consider the title of the book: Beyond Good and Evil. Our project here is meant to lie beyond convictions in dualisms, in “the belief in oppositions of values.”32 Thus, we note that the lack of “final and actual” opinions is not a Sisyphean exercise in which we fail to latch onto anything meaningful. Rather, there seems to be a similarity between the old and upcoming philosophers, that “all philosophers so far have loved their truths.” The difference is that the new types “will not be dogmatists. It would offend their pride, as well as their tase, if their truth were a truth for everyone…‘My judgment is my judgment: other people don’t have an obvious right to it too’ — perhaps this is what such philosopher of the future will say…abysses [are left] for the profound…everything rare for those who are rare themselves.”33 Simply put, we dig through caves and abysses, understanding that the conclusions we make are provisional, but nonetheless important.

Accordingly, a new philosopher’s judgment is his own judgment, not anyone else’s. As such, a central implication of the future of philosophy is that there is no generally verified foundation of claims—no definitive Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, no rudimentary First Meditation, no singular Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, no omnipotent Leviathan. All these texts can and may be true, but only so in a unique interpretive context, a cave in which the author has decided to arbitrarily pause and entertain his musings. It waits for another hermit to come along, to unearth a discovery waiting to be made about a philosopher, for that interpretation to no longer hold its dogmatic claim on the world. Thus, Nietzsche seems to ask that our new philosophers wear different masks themselves, that in engaging in the bottomless excavation of caves, they will have to embody the Kantian, the Spinozist, the Hegelian, the Wagnerian, the Platonist, and “practically everything [else]…to gaze with many eyes and consciences from the heights into eery distance, from depths up to every height, from the corner onto every expanse.”34 The hermit and the new philosopher’s originality and power stems from the qualification of their words as their ownmost, where “his very concepts will come to acquire their own twilight color.”35 This does not weaken his claims, but imbues them with even more confidence.

Now, the talk of hermits, of secrecy, of masks and caves, of esotericism, of higher and lower souls, of attempters, of twilight colors, may all be a bit general and confusing. Thus, it would do us well to invert Nietzsche’s words and observe how he uses language to saturate them with a simultaneous newfound confidence and self-aware tentativeness that is “strong and original enough to give impetus to opposed valuations and initiate a revaluation and reversal of “eternal values”; towards those sent out ahead; towards the men of the future who in the present tie the knots and gather the force that compels the will of millennia into new channels.”36 To that end, we look at Nietzsche’s will to power and the modality it is presented in—specifically, passage §211:

I am going to insist that people finally stop mistaking philosophical laborers and scientific men in general for philosophers, – that here, of all places, people be strict about giving “each his due” and not too much to the one, and much too little to the other. In the course of his education, the genuine philosopher might have been required to stand on each of the steps where his servants, the philosophical scientific laborers…But true philosophers are commanders and legislators: they say “That is how it should be!” they are the ones who first determine the “where to?” and “what for?” of people, which puts at their disposal the preliminary labor of all philosophical laborers, all those who overwhelm the past. True philosophers reach for the future with a creative hand and everything that is and was becomes a means, a tool, a hammer for them. Their “knowing” is creating, their creating is a legislating, their will to truth is – will to power. – Are there philosophers like this today? Have there ever been philosophers like this? Won’t there have to be philosophers like this? . . .37

The passage is immediately gripping if we speed-read through it (and it is hard not to do so, given Nietzsche’s incisive tone). Yet, if we heed Nietzsche’s own advice an understand that his books are “friends of the lento,” some deep insights begin to emerge.

The noticeable intimacy of the passage comes from the first few words, “I am going to insist.” Nietzsche begins many passages and sentences in this way, with others starting with “let us admit this much,” or “it seems increasingly clear to me,” or “You will have to forgive me for having discovered…”38 These personal qualifications are clearly intentional—otherwise, he would have just asserted them without the need to insist, the need to assume, the need to ask forgiveness. On one hand, he seems to be rejecting the assertive dogmatism of philosophers old by writing in such a self-interrogative tone. On another hand, he seems to be tempting and luring us into his cave, to consider his masks in some tongue-in-cheek way (I mean, who exactly is he asking for forgiveness from? Why do we need to admit anything, or insist?), to heighten our own discernment and appreciation for the fine details present in the hermit’s concealed writing, the shrouded “sign[s] and symptom[s] that first needs to be interpreted.” The slower we read, the more masks we may consider in any hermit or new philosopher or profound thinker’s writings, and perhaps Nietzsche writes in this personally qualified way to inch us towards that future.

Another quality present in §211 as well as other passages is the startling interjections of perspective changes. He starts §211 out in the first person, only to abruptly take the voice of the “true philosophers” to display how they may be “commanders and legislators.” Apparently, these figures exclaim: “That is how it should be!” and establish the ““where to?” and “what for?” of people.” These sudden insertions reflect an almost symphonic expression of language, akin to when a composer adds an occasional, abrupt break to a single chord played in unison by the orchestra before returning to the dispersed melodic theme previously played by each section.39 It reflects how he wants us to seamlessly transition into the positions of “philosophical laborers” and the “scientific men,” to successfully experience their values and transform them into “a means, a tool, a hammer” to “create values.”40 He employs this tactic elsewhere, like in §2, §37, §39, §44, §45, §62, §187, §188, §190, §199 (this list is not comprehensive). These suggest something tantamount to a constant self-assessment, to never latch onto a singular, ultimate resolvability of life, perhaps because we doubt that “a philosopher every cave does not have, must not have, an even deeper cave behind it – a more extensive, stranger, richer world above the surface, an abyss behind every ground.” It requires us to be nimble, to be confident in the values we do create and protect them, but also to “reach for the future with a creative hand,” never lacking the self-consciousness, the “will-to-power” necessary to re-imagine new forms of life based on new “tools” in the face of its nebulousness.

He ends §211 with a flurry of self-interviews that assesses what he has just said: “Are there philosophers like this today? Have there ever been philosophers like this? Won’t there have to be philosophers like this? . . .” The question marks and ellipses not only invite self-inquisition for Nietzsche’s words up until that point, but invites the reader’s self-analysis. It throws Nietzsche’s words into self-doubt, opening them up for interpretation in the context of the reader’s personal reading. To end the passage with a sense of rhetorical illusiveness—a wandering that does not tell the reader a methodology of how to reach the will to power—offers the reader to consider what he might mean by the statements he has previously put forth, and what it would mean for the significance of value if such statements were false. The way he treats the concept is through the manifestation of the “new philosophy” he has advocated for, a consideration of the masks and caves and secrecies that such a value might hold, “something of the whisper and the timid sideways glance of solitude.”41 Its evasive, tentative presentation invites the perspectivism of higher souls and lower souls; that those who see “down from above!” will possess a vantage point in which they appreciate the enormous depth and hidden complexities of what both the existence and absence of the will to power mean for them—not simply the truth and untruth of the concept.

In this respect, the mention of “masks” and “every philosophy is a foreground philosophy” in §289 has opened up a road into solitude, esotericism, higher and lower souls, the excavation of endless caves, the consideration of non-dogmatic profundities, the judgments of new philosophers, the establishment of contextual truths, into self-assessment and self-knowledge, and much more that I have not mentioned. We recognize the tentativeness in this analysis, that the lack of unquestionably true and perfectly understood beliefs are plenty in Beyond Good and Evil; especially since Nietzsche seems to be manifesting the philosophy of the future he professes. But perhaps that is the edifying process of becoming a new breed of philosopher, a hermit and marmot first, a profound thinker, a choice human, a non-dogmatist, an attempter, a free spirit: we recognize the struggle inherent in freeing ourselves from dualisms of value, but are nevertheless encouraged by the endless masks and caves to view the world in countless, overlapping pictures of interpretive values, a collection of distinct dimensions of significance present in each individual, and consequently, exploring the dynamism of the human condition.
