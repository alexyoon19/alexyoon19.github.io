The Only One Who Moves

Randall’s Pursuit of a Seagull: The Potency that Lies Within a Character’s Movement as an Emotional Mechanism

Short essay, October 2021

In the second chapter of It’s Such a Beautiful Day created by Don Hertzfeldt, our main protagonist, Bill, recalls memories of his strange family history and childhood in the midst of his failing battle with a mental illness. Specifically, the beginning of the chapter, titled I Am So Proud of You, illustrates a poignant recollection — the death of Bill’s half-brother, Randall. This scene, while just under a minute long, exemplifies the movie’s ability to evoke powerful emotions through both contrasts of motion between characters within the scenes, and in the character’s movement itself. As such, movement contributes to the creation of an emotionally powerful death scene, effectively imparting great significance onto Randall’s death within Bill’s own psyche, memory, and subsequent life.

Randall possesses aluminum hooks for arms and unstable, badly misshapen legs — thus, he cannot move normally and often stumbles when walking. Nevertheless, the moment he is allowed to attend a field trip to the beach with Bill’s class, he is mesmerized by a seagull that flies over his head, chasing after it and falling down multiple times in doing so. Eventually, Randall is unable to abandon his fixation to save his own life; he adamantly chases the seagull until reaching the ocean and subsequently drowning. These seconds contain monumental emotional power. The decisions related to Randall’s facial, bodily, and environmental movements evoke intense grief from the audience, and contribute to why this death left such an impression on Bill as well. Specifically, we see Randall’s eyes, on the bottom half of the screen, fixated onto the top half of the screen containing an open blue sky, wishfully trailing a freely flying seagull. His legs fail him from time to time, but it doesn’t matter to Randall. All he seems to want is to fulfill an impossible dream: to be emancipated from his restrictive life and misshapen body to achieve a sense of independence that does not require the presence of chaperones and protection.

The fast movement of Randall’s legs, the quickly passing sky and seagull, and his flailing arms, contrasted with his unchanging, wistful facial expression, head pinned towards the top of the screen, and repetition of the word “boon,” transmit a profound mixture of both confidence and desperation. We simultaneously feel satisfied with Randall’s newfound, unabridged bodily motion when he runs after the seagull, but are also regretful, knowing that Randall cannot ever have a life similar to this seagull that he is infatuated with. Movement is utilized within the character and in his surroundings to convey specific, complex emotions that attach consequence to a character associated with a short memory. In these ways, Randall’s death is viscerally forceful, which establishes his prominence in Bill’s mind.

While Randall’s individual motion contributes to the significant emotional power of the scene, his movement and gestures compared to others that are present in the scene demonstrate the prominence that Randall possesses within Bill’s mind and memory. In the death scene, we are introduced to many characters: Randall himself, Bill’s classmates, and the adults that are chaperoning Bill’s class’s trip to the beach. However, of these characters, Randall is the only person undertaking any significant amount of movement. The scene takes the time to show how his hook arms work, the instability of his misshapen legs, and the solitude in which he rides the bus to school, among other moments of action. As such, Randall is instantly humanized and presented as a “mobile” character, despite his malformed limbs, crooked facial features, and odd semblance. Conversely, the scene does not take the effort to establish the same level of detail or intimacy with any of the other characters; Bill’s classmates and adult chaperones do not move once in the entirety of the memory, staying in the exact same position on screen with the exact general facial expressions and body placements at both the beginning and end of the scene.

Throughout the memory, all movement is associated with Randall. The only other figure that experiences motion in this memory is the seagull that Randall naively and wishfully follows into the ocean. Randall is immediately substantiated as a dynamic agent in Bill’s memory, reinforcing his perception of Randall as a significant figure in his childhood. Specifically, Bill uses Randall’s death as a coping mechanism and explanation for his mother’s erratic, overprotective behavior and unstable mental state. Immediately after the presentation of this memory, Bill explains how his mother forced him to wear a big winter coat, helmet, and safety gloves after “losing Randall.” Thus, the loss of this spirited, peculiar young family member creates a source of grief for Bill, enabling him to rationalize his mother’s bizarre overprotectiveness and erratic habits, as well as the impact it had on him as a child. Randall serves as a personified medium for Bill’s perspective on his childhood; motion is the main mechanism in which the scene successfully plants Randall as a prominent figure in Bill’s mind.

As demonstrated, movement is an incredibly powerful tool that not only can convey complex emotions within a scene, but also substantiate the noteworthiness of a character across an entire film. Randall only has 43 seconds of screen time — a nearly negligible amount considering It’s Such a Beautiful Day’s 62-minute running time. Nevertheless, with conscious decisions to establish Randall as a non-passive agent, like detailing the way his arms and legs move, the peculiarities associated with his life, or his heartbreaking pursuit of a flying seagull, his impact on both Bill and the audience is greater than most characters in the film. Even with Bill’s recollection of many other relatives’ deaths in the subsequent minutes of the film, most of them resulting from train accidents, Randall’s passing seems more consequential. The distinct manner in which we are introduced to him and the eccentric ways in which he moves across the screen for the short amount of time he is on it indicate that Randall is a vital link in the construction of this intimate and harrowing film.
