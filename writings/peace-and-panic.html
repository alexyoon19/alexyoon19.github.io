The Same Morning, Without the Sound

Ruben’s Peace and Panic: How Sound of Metal Communicates Character Emotion and Psychological Tone for the Audience by Blending Cinematography and Sound Design

Short essay, November 2021

Our initial introduction to the main protagonist in Sound of Metal, Ruben, is filled with seemingly mundane tasks. In the moments after the first heavy metal concert of the film, the audience is presented with a sequence illustrating Ruben’s morning routine. He is seen waking up in his RV next to his girlfriend to a soft alarm, blending a green smoothie, making coffee, and working out, among other typical morning tasks. Soon after Ruben loses his hearing, we are shown a similar sequence of Ruben’s morning routine in his RV again; however, while the actions are almost identical to the previous ones when he still possessed his full auditory perception, the tone and mood of the scene are no longer calm, simple, and carefree. Namely, the manipulation of visual cinematographic elements such as camera movement, shot composition, and framing, along with the use of diegetic sound in contrasting the two scenes creates a film environment in which the audience can identify and understand Ruben’s sudden deterioration of sonic perception from a more powerfully intimate point of view. Consequently, this contrast between these two sequences establishes the foundation for how the rest of the film will present the emotional impact of Ruben’s deafness, both visually and sonically.

The manner in which the opening sequences are composed, framed, and shot enables the audience to understand and appreciate the subtleties that are associated with an emotionally stable and unbothered Ruben. When Ruben awakes next to his girlfriend in his RV following a concert the night before, the audience is greeted with tranquility — a wide shot of a calm morning, with soft sunlight beaming through the blinds of the windows. Little to no action is happening within the frame. He slowly awakes, leisurely checking if his girlfriend is still asleep as well as the state of the outside world through the blinds. The curtains behind Ruben illuminate the entire setting with a gentle yellow light, and the camera remains in a single location, moving only slightly when Ruben exits the bed. As such, the warm composition of the shot itself and the lack of cutting and camera movement throughout this scene complements the ease and repose that Ruben feels during his waking hours.

Subsequently, the film depicts Ruben beginning his usual morning routine, which will serve as an important reference point for his psychological and emotional condition when he loses his hearing. He rhythmically taps on a bottle of turmeric as he prepares a health drink for himself and his girlfriend. The close-up shot focuses specifically on this task, and makes a point to microscopically illustrate the entirety of the action — there is no other background movement or environmental distractions, only an intimate depiction of a simple task. Following the shot of the smoothie preparation, Ruben begins to blend the smoothie; this segment is also presented in close detail. All we are left to observe is Ruben’s routine, which stresses the significance of the seemingly ordinary actions he undertakes. The next few shots are incredibly similar in framing technique and duration of focus. They depict more close-up shots of a coffee maker gurgling away as it brews, Ruben working out, a soundboard being cleaned with an air duster, and a record player playing a morning soundtrack as Ruben prepares his breakfast. The stillness of the frame and the warm composition of the entire sequence both serve as an intimate visual reference for the audience of the tranquil nature of Ruben’s emotional state as he carries out his morning regimen.

In conjunction with these cinematographic decisions, the scene expertly amalgamates innovative sound design to both bolster the detail of what is happening on screen, and create the internal logic of the film that conveys both Ruben’s hearing and deaf perspective to the audience in an exhaustive and precise manner. Throughout the morning routine scene as described above, each of Ruben’s subtle actions is accompanied by prominent sounds. In the beginning of the scene, we can hear the rustling of bedsheets in imposing detail, and while it is not a particularly loud or deep noise, it is elevated to a finely detailed level that allows the audience to discern every movement that Ruben makes and associate a sound to each respective movement. Furthermore, the film holds each simple action for its full duration, so that the sustained exhibition of actions results in the accentuation of sounds such as the rhythmic tapping and distinct whirring of the blender; no other visual and auditory distractions are present. In this respect, the audience is able to associate these specific optical sequences and sonic perceptions with Ruben’s emotional and psychological calmness, as he is in an undisturbed and comfortable emotional state while undertaking particular actions and producing prominent sounds during his morning routine.

Accordingly, when the film suddenly strips away a component of a scene that we have associated with Ruben’s personal emotional stability, the impact is unsettling, terrifying, and confusing all at the same time. Specifically, once Ruben’s hearing drops out while he is setting up for the next show, the previously vibrant, prominent sounds that we associated with Ruben’s minute, yet significant actions are forcibly replaced with muffled vibrations and indecipherable undertones. We are then placed back into the familiar scene of Ruben’s morning routine. The film presents us with many of the same routine tasks as before — Ruben moves the bed sheets as he wakes up next to his girlfriend, blends another green smoothie, the coffee maker begins brewing and generating noise — and much of the close-up framing and still camera movement is identical to the first morning sequence. However, we can discern visible panic, shock, and anxiety in Ruben’s emotional state this time, as the familiar actions do not produce the expected noises. The soft rustling of bedsheets that the audience should be hearing when Ruben gets out of bed is supplanted with barely-audible vibrations. The unique, high-pitched whirring noise of the blender that we once knew is replaced with a dull, indistinguishable murmur that is almost akin to background ambience. We are shown the same close-up shot of the dripping coffee maker, but this time, it does not gurgle and produce unique dribbling sounds; it is oppressively silent.

The audience has previously been introduced to the precise noises that those respective actions produce, and was consequently prompted to connect those components to Ruben’s calm emotional state and mood. Materially, the audience expects these objects and actions to produce the same noises the second time we encounter them — especially when the film reintroduces them to us with the same framing technique and stillness it did the first time. But, as Ruben experiences deafness, the audience is forced to as well. The sound is diegetic in both mornings, since all of it comes from within the world of the film; what the film changes is whose hearing we are given, as the second morning’s sounds are heard through Ruben’s damaged ears. As such, we are better able to comprehend what Ruben is experiencing physically in real-time, and are subsequently confronted with the same emotions as Ruben that result from the sudden diminution in auditory perception: fear, surprise, and desolation.

Through this presentation of sound and an equally potent use of the visual element, the conflict that happens in Ruben’s brain — of expecting to see and hear something, but not experiencing the latter — also ensues in our own heads. The sensorially harsh contrast in the juxtaposition of these two scenes further indicates how the film will handle the progressive deterioration of Ruben’s hearing throughout the rest of the story as well. It will not warn the audience when the shift will happen from the deaf Ruben’s perspective to a hearing third person’s perspective; rather, the shift will happen as quickly as it did in this first instance, where the silence and close-up framing will signify Ruben’s sensory impressions. Auditory information will be lost in a split second, and the audience will have to deal with the visceral and emotional panic in the theater, just as Ruben has to in the universe of the film.

The two morning routine scenes demonstrate how the technique of blending shot composition, camera framing and movement, and sound design is used to more potently and personally convey a character’s emotional state, mood, and future expectations to the audience. These sequences establish how the audience will interact with different perspectives throughout the rest of the movie — switching back and forth between close-up and wide shots, as well as the interchange between the muffled sounds that Ruben hears from his perspective and the clear sounds that an omniscient third-person would perceive. Fundamentally, these two morning sequences in Sound of Metal present the dichotomy between Ruben’s states of peace and panic by utilizing cinematic elements — and the rest of the story effectively strives to redefine Ruben’s peace and panic that we are introduced to in these opening minutes.
