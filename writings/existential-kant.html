Why Should Duty Move Us?

The Existential Kant: Making Sense of Pure Practical Reason

Essay for Robert Pippin’s seminar Kant on Moral Meaning, University of Chicago. March 2024.

We are quite familiar with the Kantian account presented in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals: that the “search for and establishment of the supreme principle of morality” must be determined by pure reason only. This supreme principle of morality—the “form of rationality” as such—that determines what we ought to do as rational beings in any situation is the famous Categorical Imperative, in which a principle of moral action must be practically universalizable into a substantive and normative moral command. But to have moral worth and value, acting in accordance with the moral law must be done from “duty itself”, since such moral worth is only found “in the maxim in accordance with which [an action] is decided upon, and therefore does not depend upon the realization of the object of the action but merely upon the principle of volition in accordance with which the action is done.”1 Actions grounded in this supreme principle of morality is where the moral worth is found, and determines a good will.

This unconditional obligation and subjection to the moral law for the self-legislating human—that action must be taken on the basis of respect for the moral law—is what Kant labels as the doctrine of the fact of reason (factum der Vernunft) in his next major project on moral theory, the Critique of Practical Reason. While he hints in the Groundwork that reason “cognizes its highest practical vocation in the establishment of a good will…fulfilling an end which in turn only reason determines,” it is in the second Critique that he explicitly and more deeply explores this freely self-relating and self-lawgiving nature of reason.2 Namely, the moral law seems to occupy a unique existence; the possibility of willing a moral law at all through reason is “merely problematic,” in that it is a hypothetical and speculative thought, yet it is simultaneously “unconditionally commanded as a law” independent of experiential or external influence and “not a precept in accordance with which an action by which a desired effect is possible should be done.”3 Thus, reason’s creation of the unconditional moral law determines its immediate necessity for any rational will “capable of actions in accordance with principles”: uncaused causality, or freedom. Pure reason is practical, insofar as it regulates our rational lives at a superordinate, supersensible level, authoring and willing the categorical imperative, often referred to as the form of pure practical reason. As our consciousness of the moral law is not an analytic proposition based on pure or empirical Kantian intuitions, it “forces itself upon us of itself as a synthetic a priori proposition…it announces itself as originally lawgiving.”4 In other words, as far as we are rational beings who are autonomous, the fact of reason is that we cannot but be committed to the primacy of pure practical reason. Autonomous self-legislation of moral action is a necessary commitment for us, as rational beings, such that “freedom and unconditional practical law reciprocally imply each other.”5 The point Kant makes doubles as humanity’s commitment to an unconditional, absolute value of autonomous life as much as it is a point about the metaphysical status of morality.

This “impossibility of the denial of freedom” in our moral vocation seems a fine rationalist account, until we consider how it is embedded within the rest of Kant’s ethical oeuvre—the part that focuses on the rational agent’s relationship with the rest of his faculties and the normative actualization of such faculties in the wold. Specifically, Kant admits that we are finite, sensible, contingent beings, subject to material principles and the faculty of desire. Before his establishment of the fact of reason in the second Critique, he says the following: “All practical principles that presuppose an object (matter) of the faculty of desire as the determining ground of the will are, without exception, empirical and can furnish no practical laws” and more specifically, “a principle that is based only on the subjective condition of receptivity to a pleasure or displeasure…can, accordingly, never furnish a practical law.”6 Taken in conjunction with the fact that the moral law must be commanded as a practical law in the Kantian framework, he seems to say that our endless and contingent desires, pleasures, and displeasures cannot even enter the realm of self-legislated autonomy, given that such contingencies can never be willed into an unconditional practical law. In other words, in exercising our pure reason, we cannot just act rationally in response to our various external constraints if we are to be moral, self-legislating beings. Yet, even Kant admits that it is nonetheless a fact of life that we are subject to these external constraints we seek to satisfy. Much of them are uncontrollable and inescapable, yet powerfully motivating in human life. Such a determining ground of action is what Kant labels in a similar manner to our familiar supreme principle of morality: “all material principles, which place the determining ground of choice in the pleasure or displeasure to be felt in the reality of some object, are wholly of the same kind insofar as they belong without exception to the principle of self-love.”7

It is not difficult to see that the intimations of a paradox, or at least the overtones of a theory suspicious of itself, is emerging. On the one hand, we are creatures who are prone to pursue maxims of self-love, seeking to satisfy our own contingent ends, but at the same time, as rational beings, we cannot be but rationally committed to the moral law—a moral law that cannot be contingent on anything. The natural point of progression seems to be an imposing, rationalist view of life. As we cannot be truly self-legislating, truly autonomous, truly self-normative when our actions are pursued in response to whatever dependent ends we want, it seems only logical that we are only truly autonomous if the unconditional moral law, as the universalizable maxims, satisfies only the formal nature of reason as such, “even if this should be combined with many infringements upon the ends of inclination.”8

But how do we make sense of this unavoidable Kantian commitment with such paradoxical implications? If, as uncaused causality, human reason thrusts itself onto us as an absolute, categorical imperative, it seems to lay in perpetual conflict with the sensible, instinctual parts of ourselves. Our egoistic, self-absorbed, self-loving, desire-satisfying selves must be kept in constant check by the formal lawgiving nature of our reason, with the latter given a Sisyphean task of regulation, imposing its obligatory will on the former to ensure that our actions stem “from duty alone.” The logical conclusion here should then be that each of us ought to perform a “universalizability test” in every course of action, constantly and rigorously assessing its morality by rejecting the contradictions that arise. Formulated in this manner, the implications we have drawn from the Kantian position (one regularly taught in introductory philosophy courses) is characterizable in two ways: utterly exhausting and opaquely idealistic, devoid of the deliberative considerations we encounter in life. It is no wonder Hegel labeled Kantian morality as an “empty formalism.”9 I would like to suggest that one of the greatest minds of the past millennium would not have been advocating for a theoretical philosophy that would imply such an unattractive picture, stopping short of making fuller sense of the theory he created. In considering the deeper gradations of Kant’s larger corpus, ones that are not encapsulated in a single sentence like the categorical imperative, a fuller picture of moral experience emerges, with different implications and more inviting characterizations than the above.

Perhaps we ought to begin by re-characterizing the Kantian question to a more aesthetic one: what does the Kantian moral life look like, and how do we make sense of that picture? To be sure, I am not seeking a rebuild of Kantian morality from purely aesthetic foundations and principles; he firmly rejects this project as in the Metaphysics of Morals:

But in fact no moral principle is based, as people sometimes suppose, on any feeling whatsoever…If one departs from this principle and begins with sensibly dependent or pure aesthetic or even moral feeling (with what is subjectively rather than objectively practical); if, that is, one begins with the matter of the will, the end, instead of with the form of the will, the law, in order to determine duties on this basis, then there will indeed be no metaphysical first principles of the doctrine of virtue, since feeling, whatever may arouse it, always belongs to the order of nature.10

The task, rather, is to figure some way to interpret our sensible natures, our moral intuitions, our actualized feelings, as a part of the Kantian language encountered in both the Groundwork and the second Critique. Specifically, Kant has talked about the form of morality, the form of law, and the form of pure reason, which are all decidedly generic modes of thought and logical power, yet to be actualized in my moral life. They have not yet fully actualized themselves in full content, and we look to Kant’s Metaphysics of Morals and Critique of Judgment to make it actual, rather than simply accusing Kant of the rigoristic, empty intellectualism that he is at times charged with.

From this angle, we immediately recognize the aesthetic considerations Kant made about how the objective moral law for a rational being may appear as “subjective conditions of receptiveness” for me, the contingent individual living a finite life in a sensible world. He labels these as aesthetic preconditions [Aesthetische Vorbegriffe], which “lie at the basis of morality…All of them are natural predispositions of the mind (praedispositio) for being affected by concept of duty, antecedent predispositions on the side of feeling [ästhetisch]….every man has them, and it is by virtue of them that he can be put under obligation.”11 Thus, the possibility of the obligatory nature of the form of pure practical reason in an actualized life arises from aesthetic preconditions, because we realize these aesthetic states “from consciousness of a moral law.”12

The most primary of these predispositions is what Kant labels moral feeling. In the simplest sense, this is perhaps the Kantian name to what many of our parents taught us as children: if you do something right, you feel good inside; if you do something awful, you feel bad inside. More concretely, moral feeling is “the susceptibility to feel pleasure or displeasure merely from being aware that our actions are consistent with or contrary to the law of duty. Every determination of choice proceeds from the representation of a possible action to the deed through the feeling of pleasure of displeasure,” similar to the principle of self-love.13 It is important to note though that this feeling is a precondition, a fact of existence: “since any consciousness of obligation depends upon moral feeling to make us aware of the constraint present in the thought of duty, there can be no duty to have moral feeling or acquire it; instead every man (as a moral being) has it in him originally.”14 Even from this concept alone, the seemingly opaque, intellectual rigorism present in the Groundwork begins to dissipate; the formalized nature of the moral law more seamlessly fits into perceptual experience, a sensible manifestation stemming from the enabling power of pure practical reason.

Pivotally, Kant also assigns some normative authority to moral feeling, that we should not just passively feel it and react to it, but as a part of our moral vocation, we ought to tend to it with continuous and deep care over the course of our lives: “obligation with regard to moral feeling can be only to cultivate it and to strengthen it through wonder at its inscrutable source.”15 Moral sensibilities are not thoughtless corporeal reactions towards the moral law, but a constantly adjusting, apperceptive actualization of our being actively aware of the moral weight in our actions over time. When I ought to study for my final exams but rather end up pulling up Hulu on my computer to watch an episode of Rick and Morty, I am aware of the normativity of the action. I do not just find myself lounging around on my couch with the television on reacting to perceptual cues of the situation; I am attuned to the normative commands of the moment and recognize the shortcomings in my action, aware of “the moral vital force” and why it “excites this feeling.”16 Under such a picture, the initial rigid separability of normative self-legislation stemming from pure practical reason and feeling stemming from self-love in actuality seems a unitary phenomenon. The unconditional moral law and our sensible natures are inseparable, like the eye and the capacity to see in Aristotle’s De Anima: “suppose that the eye were an animal — sight would have been its soul.”17 Much like how if an eye would not be much of an eye if it were dead and no longer carried the power of sight, the moral law finds its completion in the actualization of it in the sensible distinctions of life.

This account of Kant seems to give us confidence that our moral vocation, which again Kant says that we cannot but be committed to, is not the life of conflict that the soul-above-body or reason-regulating-senses dichotomy suggests. But if we consider our potential for action in an apperceptive way, and if everyones has sensibilities like moral feeling and conscience “within him originally…an unavoidable fact”, while at the same time our consciousness of the binding nature of moral law “forces itself upon us of itself as a synthetic a priori proposition,” we come across another, more existential question: what kind of sensible beings are we that the categorical imperative is an imperative? More generally, why should I be committed to this end at all? Even within the holism of formal existence and lived experience as presented above, we still do not seem to possess a centrality or grounding of moral life that can give us confidence in its pursuit—not just a lack of perpetual conflict while participating in the moral life, but belief in pursuing the moral life as such. Kant’s answers thus far have seems similar to appeals to the noumenal world—non-sensible, special modes of intuition like freedom and pure practical reason—outside our experiential realm. To stop here, however, would seem a suspicious foundation: Kant is essentially telling us that we are unavoidably committed to our moral vocation because of value derived from a realm beyond the phenomenal world, but as phenomenal beings, this explanation cannot fully make that pursuit and commitment salient or significant to us.

This gives us a chance to return to Kant’s sensible and aesthetic language in the Metaphysics of Morals. Kant tells us we should “strengthen [moral feeling] through wonder at its inscrutable source,” and that these feelings are excited by a “moral vital force.” More specifically, in speaking about the aesthetic precondition of conscience, this appeal to an aesthetic value and subjective judgment becomes stronger and more immediately salient:

while I can indeed be mistaken at times in my objective judgment as to whether something is a duty or not, I cannot be mistaken in my subjective judgment as to whether I have submitted it to my practical reason (here in its role as judge) for such a judgment; for if I could be mistaken in that, I would have made no practical judgment at all, and in that case there would be neither truth nor error…It is incumbent upon him only to enlighten his understanding in the matter of what is or is not duty; but when it comes, or has come, to a deed, conscience speaks involuntarily and unavoidably.18

This perceptive salience of morality can be more fully explored in the Critique of Judgment, specifically in delineations of judgments of sublimity. These are pleasures “that arises only indirectly…it is produced by the feeling of a momentary inhibition of the vital forces followed immediately by an outpouring of them that is all the stronger.”19

But importantly, like moral feeling that is “induced most intensely in its purity by a merely rational representation,” the sublime similarly “concerns only ideas of reason.”20 Sublimity is a feeling of negative pleasure; a universally valid moment defined by the initial apprehension and subsequent feeling of superiority in our power of reason when experiencing the incommensurate nature of an object, like a powerful storm or vast mountain range. As Kant puts it, in such a sensible experience, an inversion occurs, where “the mind is induced to abandon sensibility and occupy itself with ideas containing a higher purposiveness.”21 It becomes clear that sublimity does not stem from nature itself; it is a feeling, induced by “the irresistibility of nature's might”, while also reflexively being a feeling that elevates the standing of reason in our mind. This alternation between such fear of powerful reality and elevation of pure reason “makes us, considered as natural beings, recognize our physical impotence,” while simultaneously revealing “an ability to judge ourselves independent of nature, and…a superiority over nature that is the basis of a self-preservation quite different in kind from the one that can be assailed and endangered by nature outside us…Hence if in judging nature aesthetically we call it sublime, we do so not because nature arouses fear, but because it calls forth our strength (which does not belong to nature).”22

And so, in this picture, the primacy of commitment to the moral law is not a clashing of sensible pursuits with our ultimate rational one, and as rather the value of the latter being reinforced with experiences of the former. Even though we are often dominated by natural power, ones that are at times incommensurately powerful may remind us that the sensible experience is not just sensible—there is a rational form behind it, a supersensible, moral vocation that strengthens our existence in our phenomenal world. That feeling of sublimity is admittedly quite mysterious in that it goes beyond its natural characteristics to overwhelm us. Yet, because there is no threat in experiencing it, the result is reason’s self-elevation; it raises us up in feelings of confidence that our supersensible vocation will not be adversely affected by natural power, precisely because the value of that vocation lies beyond that realm. In this respect, we experience self-elevation in the face of humbling, a self-respect. The inevitable pursuit of our moral law in a sensible world offers a chance of respect and strength, an “aesthetic device that still points to a moral sense.”23 In pursuing the moral life in our desire-driven, sensibly-contingent world, we move towards recognizing and actualizing a special self-elevation in that world: “the law within him unavoidably forces from him respect for his own being, and this feeling (which is of a special kind) is the basis of certain duties, that is, of certain actions that are consistent with his duty to himself. It cannot be said that a man has a duty of respect toward himself, for he must have respect for the law within himself in order even to think of any duty whatsoever.”24

So, when Kant tells us that the only obligation we have towards moral feeling is the obligation to cultivate it, it is perhaps to attune our sensibility to greater sensitivity of the moral law. Specifically, we all have our own, material, contingent ends, but those ends do not have to be in constant battle with the supersensible, moral one. We may cultivate interpretive finesse such that the interoperation of the moral law is not limited or singular, regressing into the impositionist Kantian view; in experiencing art, nature, literature, or even a physics professor speaking about the vast depths of the universe, we cultivate our moral sensibilities in a way that excites feelings of confidence and elevation in our self-legislative force. Induced by confrontations with sublimity, a cultivating experience imbues us with moral feeling and ultimately, respect for our being—we may find the confidence to pursue our supersensible vocation as a valuable end, because it elevates the significance of our existence.
