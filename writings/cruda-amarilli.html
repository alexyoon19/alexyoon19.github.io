An Echo With No Listener

Music in “Cruda Amarilli”: Critical in Fully Understanding its Essential Themes and Emotions

Short note, January 2022

Monteverdi’s “Cruda Amarilli” presents a storytelling method in which music does not simply enhance the presentation of character emotion and textual substance, but becomes an inseparable part of these two narrative components. Without the musical element, much of the nuance present in the internal discord, external conflict, and interpersonal relationships within the poem would be lost. “Cruda Amarilli” is able to exhibit a multi-layered, subtle conveyance of a textually complex poem through the use of dissonance and rhythmic repetition, which more effectively communicates the poem’s themes of anguish, hopelessness, and unattainable love.

One of the most noticeable musical components in Monteverdi’s madrigal is the consistent use of pitch dissonance. In carefully selected passages, Monteverdi uses harmonic tension between the vocalists to express Mirtillo’s intense dejection. These moments of extreme emotion are particularly evident when the words “ahi lasso! amaramente insegni” and “ma de l’aspido sordo e più sorda e più fera e più fugace,” which textually indicate Mirtillo’s grief, are sung. These lines follow passages of vocal harmony, in which the lyrics denote Mirtillo’s physical attraction to Amarilli. This juxtaposition of dissonance and dejected lyrics to harmony and pleasing lyrics represents the incompatible dualism between Mirtillo’s pain due to his inability to be with Amarilli and his simultaneous love for her. Thus, the musical and vocal component to the poem intensifies Mirtillo’s sentiments regarding Amarilli’s forced rejection of his love. The discordance in the melody the audience hears sonically emulates Mirtillo’s inability to accept that his love for Amarilli is not to be, further representative of the “cruelness” he feels in his relationship with Amarilli.

Moreover, repetition is utilized to indicate another sentiment that permeates the poem: hopelessness. Throughout the madrigal, when not singing together (often in dissonance), the vocalists enter certain phrases and musical passages at different times, and repeatedly sing certain lines staggered with one another. This technique deepens the audience’s understanding of the text. When the poem mentions the name “Amarilli” and in the line “i’ mi morrò tacendo,” the vocalists’ continuous and softening repetition imitates an echo that rings throughout an empty hall. Namely, while the text only calls for a single vocalization of each line or word, the musical representation incorporates repetition with varying vocal pitches, accentuating certain lines and words that are important in the poem. This separated repetition is especially apparent in the last line, “i’ mi morrò tacendo,” where the word “silence” is mentioned, almost as if Mirtillo’s hopeless cries and jumbled thoughts regarding his unattainable love reverberate through the silence, without a listener to hear him. In this manner, Mirtillo’s hopelessness and confusion are communicated to the audience through musical technique.

Thus, the madrigal is not just a tool to communicate the poem; it serves both as a medium and catalyst for greater emotional messaging, pivotal to fully understanding the story and nuance that the poem aims to depict. The music serves as the cohesive cement that binds the story, characters, and emotions into a single, perceptible creation that more effectively relays the essence of the poem.
